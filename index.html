Welcoming Brazilian &
Portuguese-speaking newcomers

This toolkit is here to help teachers get to know the linguistic, cultural, and educational backgrounds Brazilian students bring with them, and to build classrooms where Portuguese-speaking newcomers can thrive.

It's built around what students already know, not what they're missing. The pages that follow walk through research-based strategies, ways to partner with families, and honest questions worth asking about our own assumptions.

Cultural foundations

Brazil is one of the world's most culturally diverse nations, shaped by Indigenous peoples, Portuguese colonization, African heritage, and immigration from Europe, Asia, and the Middle East.

Many Brazilian families have recently relocated to Pennsylvania for employment, education, and family reunification. Brazilian students bring valuable cultural knowledge, multilingual abilities, and resilience into schools.

Language

  • Official language: Portuguese (not Spanish)
  • Romance language family
  • Roman alphabet with accent marks (á, é, í, ó, ú)
  • The cedilla (ç) and nasal vowels (ã, õ)

Phonological characteristics

  • Nasal vowel sounds
  • Different pronunciation of R and L
  • Different stress patterns than English
  • Students may add vowel sounds to English words ending in consonants

Cultural values

  • Strong family relationships
  • Respect for teachers
  • Community and collaboration
  • Music, dance, soccer, Carnival, Festa Junina, and storytelling

Student assets

  • Multilingualism
  • Adaptability
  • Strong family support
  • Creativity
  • Global perspectives
  • Cultural pride

Classroom impact

Every Brazilian newcomer has a unique educational background. Teachers should begin with curiosity rather than assumptions.

According to Wright (2019), conversational English (BICS) develops much faster than academic English (CALP). Students may appear fluent socially while still needing significant language supports in academic settings.

Strengths-based instruction

  • Build on Portuguese literacy
  • Teach English–Portuguese cognates
  • Use visuals, graphic organizers, and sentence frames
  • Encourage bilingual note-taking and translanguaging
  • Promote cooperative learning and peer discussion

Family engagement

  • Provide translated communication
  • Use interpreters
  • Value Portuguese as a home language
  • Invite families to participate in school activities

“How can I build instruction around everything this student already knows?” — rather than “How can this student catch up?”

The bias audit

Educators should critically examine assumptions that may negatively affect multilingual learners.

Common biases and asset-based alternatives

Brazilian students speak Spanish.
01
Instead
Recognize Portuguese as a distinct language and provide appropriate supports.
Limited English means limited intelligence.
02
Instead
Separate language proficiency from academic ability and allow multiple ways to demonstrate learning.
Parents are uninvolved.
03
Instead
Remove communication barriers through translation and flexible engagement opportunities.
Students should only speak English.
04
Instead
Encourage bilingualism and translanguaging to strengthen learning.
Students should assimilate quickly.
05
Instead
Celebrate multilingual identities while helping students navigate school culture.

Curriculum and assessment

  • Include Brazilian perspectives and authors
  • Reduce assessment bias through visuals, extended time, and multiple assessment formats

Teacher reflection

Regularly ask whether instructional decisions are based on assumptions or on individual student strengths.

Community action plan

Goal: build authentic partnerships with Brazilian and Portuguese-speaking families.

Action steps

  • Translate important documents into Portuguese
  • Use professional interpreters
  • Host newcomer orientation sessions
  • Develop multilingual welcome guides
  • Celebrate Brazilian culture throughout the year
  • Connect families with local community organizations
  • Provide professional development on multilingual learners and culturally responsive teaching

Communication barriers & solutions

Limited EnglishPortuguese translations
Unfamiliar school systemFamily orientations
Work schedulesFlexible meeting times
TransportationVirtual conferences

Success indicators

  • Increased family engagement
  • Improved student confidence
  • Positive school climate
  • Strong school–community partnerships
  • Recognition of multilingualism as an asset

Schools share responsibility for creating welcoming, inclusive environments that honor students' languages, cultures, and identities.
